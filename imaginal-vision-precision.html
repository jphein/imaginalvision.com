What does "imaginal" mean?

The word imaginal was coined by Henry Corbin (1903–1978), the French philosopher and scholar of Islamic mysticism, in his landmark 1964 essay Mundus Imaginalis, or the Imaginary and the Imaginal. He needed a word for something the Islamic philosophers (Suhrawardi, Ibn Arabi) treated as ontologically real — an intermediate realm between the physical/sensory world and the purely intellectual. He derived "imaginal" from the Latin imago the same way "original" derives from origo, giving it a dignified, precise meaning.

"Imaginal" is explicitly not "imaginary." Imaginary means unreal, made-up, fanciful. Imaginal means a genuine mode of perception — a way of knowing through images that is as real as sight or reason.

"An image is not what one sees but the way in which one sees."

Edward Casey, philosopher (working with James Hillman)

James Hillman (1926–2011), founder of archetypal psychology, built directly on Corbin's work. Hillman replaced the term "unconscious" with imagination, centering images as the basis of the psyche. His definition of soul: "By 'soul' I mean the imaginative possibility in our natures." Scholar Walter Odajnyk argued Hillman should have called his school "imaginal psychology" rather than archetypal psychology, since it is fundamentally based on his understanding of the imagination.

"The datum with which archetypal psychology begins is the image."

James Hillman, Re-Visioning Psychology (1975)

In expressive arts therapy, the imaginal is foundational. Shaun McNiff, who founded the field at Lesley University in 1974 and wrote Art as Medicine: Creating a Therapy of the Imagination, built his entire method on dialoguing with images and treating creative works as autonomous imaginal presences. The core principle: "Caring for the imaginal is caring for the psyche."

Why "Imaginal Vision"?

1

The philosophy literally pairs these two words

Edward Casey, working alongside Hillman, defined the imaginal as "not what one sees but the way in which one sees" — a form of vision. Hillman's book is called Re-Visioning Psychology. The mundus imaginalis requires its own faculty of perception — a form of vision that is neither sensory sight nor abstract intellect. "Imaginal Vision" isn't a marketing invention. It's the actual concept.

2

It carries clinical credibility

"Imaginal" is a recognized term in depth psychology, Jungian analysis, and expressive arts therapy. Therapists, counselors, and clinicians will immediately recognize it. It signals that this isn't just a craft project — it's grounded in a real therapeutic tradition going back through Hillman, Corbin, and Jung.

3

It's accessible to clients too

Even if someone has never heard the word "imaginal," it sounds like imagination and vision — both warm, inviting, creative words. There's no jargon barrier. A client can hear "Imaginal Vision" and think "this is about seeing things in a new way through creativity." That's exactly right.

4

It works for both sides of the business

Therapy sessions (online & in-person): "Imaginal Vision" describes the therapeutic process itself — using creative expression to see what was previously unseen, to give form to inner experience.

5

It's distinctive and ownable

While "imaginal" appears in some therapy contexts, "Imaginal Vision" as a combined brand is unique. It's not generic like "Creative Arts Therapy" or "Art Healing Studio." It's a name people remember, ask about, and look up.

6

It sounds beautiful

Say it out loud: "Imaginal Vision." Five syllables. Musical. The soft 'g' in imaginal flows into the clarity of vision. It sounds like what it is — something between a dream and a destination.

The lineage

Henry Corbin (1903–1978)

French philosopher who coined mundus imaginalis in his 1964 essay. Distinguished the imaginal (an ontologically real intermediate realm accessed through creative imagination) from the imaginary (mere fantasy). Derived from the Islamic philosophical concepts of alam al-mithal (world of archetypal images) and barzakh (the interworld).

Carl Jung (1875–1961)

Developed active imagination between 1913–1916 — a technique where unconscious contents are engaged through images, painting, sculpting, writing, and dialogue. Jung held that psyche and image are one. Active imagination is the direct precursor to all imaginal therapeutic work.

James Hillman (1926–2011)

Founded archetypal psychology. Replaced "unconscious" with imagination as the basis of psyche. His book Re-Visioning Psychology (1975) explicitly frames the psychological project as one of transformed vision. His concept of the "imaginal ego" means: maturity is not control but depth of perception, not distance from imagination but deeper participation in it.

Shaun McNiff & Expressive Arts Therapy (1974–present)

McNiff founded the first integrated expressive arts therapy program at Lesley University in 1974. His method — developed in Art as Medicine: Creating a Therapy of the Imagination — involves dialoguing with images and treating creative works as autonomous imaginal presences. Together with Paolo Knill and Stephen Levine, he built an intermodal approach where the imaginal is central to theory and practice.
